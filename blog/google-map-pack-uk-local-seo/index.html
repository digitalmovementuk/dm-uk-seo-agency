How to win the Google map pack in your town

Three businesses get shown. Everyone else sits behind a “more places” link almost nobody taps. For a plumber, a clinic or a solicitor those three slots are often the whole month’s enquiries — and the work to reach them is more specific than most people are told.

By Raoul (Alex) Müller, Founder · 28 July 2026

The short answer

You win the Google map pack by being the most relevant, most complete and best-reviewed business near the person searching. In practice that means a fully filled Google Business Profile in the right primary category, a business name, address and phone number that match everywhere online, a steady flow of genuine reviews that describe the work you did, and real location pages on your website behind it. Proximity to the searcher is the one factor you cannot change.

01 01 — The prize

Why three slots decide the month

Search for “emergency electrician near me” on a phone and the map appears above the ordinary results. Three businesses are listed with a rating, a distance and a call button. Most people tap one of the three and stop.

That block is the map pack. It is fed by Google Business Profile rather than by your website directly, which is why a company can rank on page one of the normal results and still be invisible on the map — and why the reverse happens too.

Google ranks these results on relevance, distance and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile matches what was typed. Distance is how close you are. Prominence is how well known and well regarded you appear, drawn from reviews, links, mentions and ordinary search.

Two of those three you can work on directly. That is the whole job.

Relevance · Distance · ProminenceYou can improve two of them. Distance is fixed by where you are.
02 02 — The profile

Your Google Business Profile is the page you are ranking

Treat it like a landing page, not a directory entry. These are the fields that change outcomes, roughly in the order they matter.

1 · Category

Get the primary category exactly right

This field carries more weight than anything else on the profile. Choose the category that names what you actually do most, not the broader or more impressive one — “emergency plumber” rather than “contractor”, “orthodontist” rather than “dentist”. Add secondary categories for your other real services. Useful check: look at what the three businesses winning your search have chosen.

2 · Address

Set the address or service area honestly

If customers come to you, use the street address. If you travel to them and have no premises they can visit, hide the address and set a service area. Never use a virtual office, a mailbox or a relative’s house to appear in a town you are not in. Fake locations are the commonest cause of a suspended profile, and suspensions take weeks to overturn if they are overturned at all.

3 · Services

List every service, with a real description

Add each service as its own entry with two or three sentences in plain words. This is one of the few places you can tell Google, in your own language, the specific jobs you take on. Empty service lists are common, and they cost relevance on the long, specific searches that convert best.

4 · Hours

Keep hours, holidays and contact routes accurate

A profile showing “closed” when you are open loses the call outright. Set special hours for bank holidays and Christmas before they arrive. Make sure the phone rings a person, the website link goes to a relevant page rather than the homepage, and any booking or messaging option is one somebody watches.

5 · Photos

Add photos regularly, not once

Recency matters more than volume. Real photographs of the team, the premises, the vans and finished work do more than stock imagery, and profiles with fresh images tend to earn more clicks from the same position. A handful a month is a fair habit.

6 · Questions

Use the questions and answers section

Anyone can post a question on your profile, and anyone can answer it — including a competitor. Get there first. Post the questions you are asked on the phone each week and answer them properly. It is also increasingly the text an assistant quotes about your business.

03 03 — Consistency

NAP consistency, and how many citations you really need

NAP stands for name, address and phone number. Those three facts should be identical everywhere they appear online, down to the abbreviation and the punctuation. “Suite 4, 12 High Street” and “12 High St, Ste 4” are one place to you and two places to a machine trying to confirm you exist.

The usual damage comes from history. An old office. A phone number from before the mobile. A trading name that changed. A directory that scraped the wrong details years ago and repeats them still. Each adds doubt, and doubt costs prominence.

The fix is a tidy-up, not a shopping spree. Find every listing that mentions your business, correct the wrong ones and claim the ones you never claimed. Most local businesses need a short list done properly: Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, the main UK aggregators, your trade or professional body, and the two or three directories that matter in your sector. Fifty scraped listings on sites nobody visits give the wrong details fifty places to live.

04 04 — Reviews

Reviews are the lever most businesses under-use

Rating, count, recency and wording all feed the map pack, and the wording is the part almost nobody works on.

01

Ask at the moment of relief

Ask when the customer is most pleased: the job finished, the leak stopped, the case settled. Ask in person, then send the link by text from the driveway. A process that asks every customer beats a campaign that asks fifty at once, which looks abnormal and can be filtered.

02

Reviews that describe the work rank better

“Great service, thanks” tells Google nothing. “Replaced our boiler in Hitchin the same day” tells it what you do and where. You cannot script a review, but you can ask the customer to mention what the job was. Google often lifts that wording into the justification line under your listing.

03

Reply to all of them, including the bad ones

A calm, specific reply to a poor review does more good than the review does harm. Prospective customers read the response more closely than the complaint. Never argue, never mention private details, take it offline.

04

Never buy reviews, and never gate them

Buying reviews breaches Google’s policies and UK consumer protection rules. Asking only the happy ones, sometimes sold as “review gating”, is also against the rules. Both are detectable, and recovering costs far more than the reviews were worth.

05 05 — Location pages

Location pages that earn their place

Your website supports the profile. Prominence draws partly on how well you rank in ordinary search, so a strong page about the service in that town helps the map result there.

What does not help is the spun template: twelve pages identical except for a town name and a stock photograph. Google treats those as doorway pages, they are easy to detect, and they read badly to the human who lands on one.

A location page worth publishing contains things that could only be written about that place. What you do there and how often. Parking, access, the nearest station. Response times from your depot. Conservation areas or housing stock that change the job. Named team members who cover it. Real reviews from that area, quoted with permission. If you cannot fill a page with specific detail about a town, do not publish one yet.

One page per town you genuinely serve will outperform thirty published in a weekend. That is the principle underneath the rest of a proper SEO programme: fewer pages that deserve to exist.

06 06 — The limits

The one thing you cannot fix, and what to do instead

Distance is not negotiable. The map pack recentres on wherever the searcher is standing, so a business in one part of a city will struggle to appear for someone searching from the other side. Tools reporting a single position for a whole town hide this; the honest view is a grid of positions across the area.

What you can do is strategic rather than technical. Be excellent in the radius you can realistically win, usually smaller than owners would like. Open a second location only if you can staff a real one. Use paid search to cover the outer areas the map will not carry you into, since ads are not bound by proximity the same way.

Your profile data also travels further than the map now. Ask an assistant for a supplier in a town and the answer is often built from Business Profile details, reviews and your website together — which is why the work that gets you cited in AI answers overlaps so heavily with this list.

Grid, not rankAsk for local rankings as a map grid. A single number for a whole town tells you almost nothing.
07 07 — Order of work

A sensible order for the first ninety days

Weeks 1–2
Claim and verify the profile. Fix the primary category, address or service area, hours, phone and website link. Correct every wrong citation you find.
Weeks 3–4
Write out every service with a proper description. Add photographs. Seed the questions section with the questions you genuinely get asked.
Weeks 5–8
Put a review request into the job process, so every customer is asked at the right moment by the person who did the work. Reply to all of them within two days.
Weeks 9–12
Build the first location page for your strongest town, with detail that could only be written about that place. Then measure with a grid, not a single rank.

Nothing on that list is clever, which is the point: the map pack rewards a business that has done the ordinary things completely, and most have not. The last piece is making sure the calls get answered and followed up. Capturing every enquiry with its source attached is what tells you whether any of this worked.
