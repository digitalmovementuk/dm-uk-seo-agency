01 01 — The market

A city that mostly sells advice

Leeds is one of the largest centres for financial and professional services outside London. Banking operations, building societies, insurers, accountancy firms and a very large legal sector cluster around Wellington Place, Park Square and the city core. Alongside them sits an unusually big public-sector and health presence — NHS England is headquartered in the city at Quarry House, and a digital health and data cluster has grown around it. Add two large universities and a retail centre that pulls from across the north, and the result is an economy built on selling expertise rather than things.

That shapes search here more than any point of geography. Buyers of professional advice do not decide in an afternoon. They read, compare, check credentials, look for evidence of similar work, ask a colleague — and only then make contact, often weeks later and often by typing the firm's name directly. In that pattern a top-three position is worth far less than a set of pages that survive scrutiny: what the service covers, who does it, what it costs in broad terms, what usually goes wrong, and what proof exists that you have done it before.

It also means the enquiry rarely credits the work that earned it. Someone reads three of your articles in February and calls in April after searching your company name. Reporting that counts only what was typed at the moment of contact will conclude the content did nothing. We measure the earlier steps too — which pages the people who eventually enquired had read, and which searches first brought them in — because in a research-led market that is where the work actually happens.

Consumer-facing businesses in the same city run on the opposite clock. Conveyancing, family law, dentistry, opticians, garages, gyms and restaurants are decided quickly, close to home, and won in the three-business map result. Most Leeds firms of any size have some of each, and the common mistake is running one plan for two entirely different behaviours.

02 02 — Geography

The city region reaches further than the city

Leeds City Council covers a very large metropolitan district, and not all of it is city. Otley, Wetherby, Morley, Pudsey, Garforth and Horsforth are separate towns inside the same authority, and someone searching in Wetherby is thirteen miles from the centre — far enough that the map results they see contain a completely different set of businesses. Headingley, Chapel Allerton and Roundhay each carry their own character and their own demand.

Beyond the boundary lies the wider city region. Bradford is a major city nine miles west with a distinct identity and its own economy, and it held UK City of Culture in 2025. Wakefield, Huddersfield, Halifax and Dewsbury are established towns with their own centres. Harrogate, in North Yorkshire, is affluent, separate, and behaves as its own market. York and Selby sit at the eastern edge. None of these places thinks of itself as Leeds, and none of them searches that way.

The consequence is familiar but it bites in a particular shape here: a Leeds city-centre address gives you the centre and a ring of a few miles around it, and everything beyond that has to be earned deliberately — a page for each town you genuinely serve, and a separately verified profile wherever you have a real office. If you cannot write something true about working in Halifax, do not publish a Halifax page. The six workstreams of an SEO programme include local work precisely so this gets done properly rather than at volume.

03 03 — Regulated sectors

Law, finance and health need a different kind of care

A large share of the Leeds economy sits under a regulator, and that shapes what a website is allowed to say. Law firms are bound by SRA rules on how services and costs are presented. Financial advice and insurance fall under the FCA, where a promotion must be fair, clear and not misleading. Healthcare providers carry their own advertising restrictions. None of this prevents good search work, but it does rule out much of what agencies reach for by default.

So we write to the evidence. No implied guarantees of outcome, no invented figures dressed as research, no testimonial that says something the client could not say themselves, and no comparison claim we cannot support. Where a claim needs a source, the source goes on the page. This is slower than the alternative, and it is the version that survives a compliance review — which is the only version worth publishing.

There is an unexpected benefit. The same discipline — specific, evidenced, plainly worded — is what makes a page useful to Google and quotable by an AI assistant. Being cited by ChatGPT, Gemini and AI Overviews depends on being clear, consistent and verifiable, which is exactly what a regulator wants as well. Compliance and visibility pull in the same direction far more often than people expect.

What we will not do

Claims we decline to write

Guaranteed rankings. Guaranteed outcomes for your own clients. Invented figures presented as research. Reviews written by us. Comparison claims about named competitors that cannot be evidenced. If a proposal depends on any of these, it is not one we can deliver — and in a regulated sector it is one that can cost you a great deal more than a ranking.
