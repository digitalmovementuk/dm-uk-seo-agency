01 01 — The market

One city, two economies

Bristol supports two quite different kinds of business, and they need almost opposite search work. One sells to the world: aerospace and advanced engineering on the northern edge at Filton, semiconductor and chip design established here for decades, and a screen and animation cluster that works for commissioners anywhere. For those companies a map ranking is close to worthless. Their buyers search a capability, a standard, a credit or a specialism, from wherever they happen to be, and the work is national and international.

The other economy is the ordinary one, and it is large. A dense city centre, a busy harbourside hospitality trade, two universities, a substantial financial services presence, and the full range of trades, clinics and professional firms a city this size needs. Here proximity decides most of it, and the three-business map result above the normal listings carries a great deal of the demand.

A good many Bristol firms have a foot in each. An engineering business sells nationally but recruits locally. A design studio takes work from London and from the street outside. The two need different pages, different measurement and different timescales, so we separate them before spending anything, and sequence our SEO work around whichever will produce enquiries first.

Two directions Filton sells to the world. The harbourside sells to the street. One plan will not serve both.
02 02 — Sectors

The sectors that define the city

Four parts of the Bristol economy account for most of the work we are asked to do, and their buyers could hardly behave more differently.

01

Aerospace, defence & advanced engineering

Filton and the surrounding South Gloucestershire estates hold one of Europe's more significant aerospace clusters — Airbus, Rolls-Royce and GKN Aerospace among the names — with a deep supply chain of machinists, finishers, testers and specialist fabricators behind them. Buyers here are procurement and engineering teams. They search processes, materials, standards and approvals, from anywhere in the world, and they read specifications rather than sales copy. Accreditations, tolerances and evidence of similar work do the persuading. The word “Bristol” does very little.

02

Screen, animation & creative

The BBC's Natural History Unit made Bristol the centre of wildlife film-making, Aardman is based here, and a substantial trade of studios, post houses, sound, effects and freelancers works around them. Commissioners and producers do not search by city. They search by specialism and by credit, and they check who has done the thing before. Named credits, showreels and clearly defined services matter more than any local ranking — and increasingly so does being visible when a researcher asks an assistant for a shortlist.

03

Technology & semiconductors

Chip design and electronics have been established in the city for decades, alongside a broad software and data scene and the spin-outs around two universities. These are considered purchases, researched over weeks by people who do not care where the supplier sits. The work is depth on the problem, honest pricing signals, proof a technical buyer can verify, and optimising for AI assistants — because the shortlist an assistant produces is now often the first one a buyer sees.

04

Financial services, hospitality & local trade

Bristol also holds a serious financial services presence — Hargreaves Lansdown is headquartered here — alongside the harbourside hospitality trade, a large student population, and the usual range of clinics, trades and professional firms. This is where local search earns its money: a complete and verified Google Business Profile, business details that match everywhere they appear, genuine reviews, and a site that loads fast and puts the phone number where a thumb can reach it.

03 03 — Geography

Where Bristol actually ends

The council boundary is tight and the place is not. The built-up area runs straight out into South Gloucestershire — Filton, Patchway, Bradley Stoke and Yate — which is where much of the aerospace employment physically sits, despite everybody calling it Bristol. West and south-west, North Somerset takes in Portishead, Clevedon and Nailsea. South-east lies Bath and North East Somerset. Keynsham sits between the two cities and belongs to neither in the way people talk about it.

Then there is Bath itself. Twelve miles away, a completely separate identity, a different economy built on heritage, tourism and its own professional trade, and its own high-value market. Nobody in Bath describes themselves as being in Bristol, and treating it as an outer suburb is the quickest way to write a page that convinces nobody.

Beyond that the South West spreads thin. Gloucester and Cheltenham sit up the M5, Swindon and the Wiltshire towns lie east, and Somerset, Devon and Cornwall run south into genuinely rural territory where the nearest specialist may be an hour away — which makes those searches valuable and often far easier to win. Cardiff is roughly forty-five minutes over the Severn: a real catchment for many Bristol businesses, and a different country for anything with a regulatory or public-sector dimension.
