01 01 — The region

One conurbation, four separate markets

Birmingham is the largest local authority in the country by population, and the built-up area around it does not stop at the city boundary. The West Midlands conurbation runs on unbroken through Solihull, Sandwell, Dudley, Walsall and Wolverhampton, with Coventry a short distance east. On a map it looks like one place. In how people search, it is nothing of the sort.

The clearest case is the Black Country. Dudley, Sandwell, Walsall and Wolverhampton hold a deliberate identity of their own, and people there frequently do not describe themselves as being in Birmingham or search that way — “Black Country” is a term people genuinely type. A business chasing customers in Tipton, West Bromwich or Halesowen while targeting only the Birmingham term will lose most of that demand to firms who named the place properly.

The same pattern repeats inside the city. Sutton Coldfield only became part of Birmingham in 1974 and still behaves like a separate town, with its own centre, its own catchment and its own map results. Solihull is a separate borough entirely, and its residents rarely call themselves Birmingham. Digbeth, Edgbaston, Selly Oak, Kings Heath, Erdington and Moseley each carry demand under their own names. Coventry, twenty miles east, is a different city with a different economy and should never be swept into a Birmingham plan.

So the first job is to work out which of these places you genuinely trade in, and then to name them. Where a business has real coverage in a town, that town earns a page written about it. Where it does not, the page should not exist. Publishing one near-identical page per district with the name swapped is precisely the pattern Google's spam policy singles out, and it risks an entire site to chase places you could not serve anyway.

Name the place A large part of the region does not call itself Birmingham. Say Walsall, Dudley or Solihull — and mean it.
  • Customers in Wolverhampton, Walsall and Dudley who never use the word Birmingham.
  • Sutton Coldfield buyers who search their own town and see a different set of businesses entirely.
  • Solihull, the NEC and the airport, which sit in a separate borough with separate results.
  • Coventry, twenty miles east, treated as an outer suburb when it is a city in its own right.
  • Trade coverage claimed across the whole conurbation when the vans only cross two boroughs.
  • Manufacturers competing for local terms when their buyers are sourcing nationally.
02 02 — Industrial search

Manufacturing search looks nothing like local search

The region's industrial past is not past. Engineering, automotive supply, metal finishing, tooling, castings and precision machining still run through the West Midlands, and the Jewellery Quarter in Hockley remains a working cluster of jewellers, casters, engravers and setters rather than a heritage attraction. These businesses sell in a way that has almost nothing in common with a local trade.

A buyer sourcing a machined component does not look for an engineer in Birmingham. They search a process, a material, a tolerance, a certification or an industry, and they do it from anywhere in the country or outside it. Volumes are small — sometimes a handful of searches a month — and the contracts behind them can run to six figures. For these firms the map result is close to irrelevant, and the work is national: capability pages written precisely enough for an engineer to trust, specifications a buyer can check, accreditations named, and content that answers the technical question better than a competitor's brochure page.

The professional core is different again. Colmore Business District holds a serious concentration of accountancy, law and banking, and HSBC UK's ring-fenced bank is headquartered in the city — buyers in that market research at length, check credentials, and often arrive already holding your name. The exhibition economy around the NEC and the ICC creates a third pattern: sharp, dated demand that spikes for a few weeks around an event and then disappears. Each of the three needs a different plan, which is why our SEO services get sequenced rather than applied as a package.

Low volume, high value A handful of searches a month can be worth more than a thousand local clicks. Volume is a poor guide here.
03 03 — Approach

Map, fix, build, report

The same four stages every time, shaped around which parts of the conurbation you can genuinely win.

1 · Map

The places you actually trade in

We separate the towns you genuinely serve from the ones sitting on a coverage list because they sounded useful. That normally means naming places properly — Walsall rather than “West Midlands”, Sutton Coldfield rather than “north Birmingham” — and cutting the areas where you could not realistically win or deliver.

2 · Fix

Clear the blockers first

Technical problems come first because they are the cheapest wins available: pages Google cannot index, slow templates failing on mobile, duplicate titles and descriptions, broken redirects, and business details that disagree between the website, the Google Business Profile and the directories. In a region with this many similarly named companies, inconsistent details are a common and very quiet cause of weak local results.

3 · Build

The pages that earn enquiries

For local demand that means one honest page per town you serve, plus a complete verified profile for each real location. For technical and national demand it means capability pages an engineer would trust — processes, materials, tolerances, accreditations — and content that answers the question a buyer would otherwise have to ask on the phone.

4 · Report

Enquiries, by place and by search

Every month we show which towns produced enquiries, which searches brought them, what is still stuck and what happens next. Written in plain words and tied back to the page that created each enquiry, so the following month's budget is decided on evidence rather than instinct.
